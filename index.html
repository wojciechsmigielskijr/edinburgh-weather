A dated call beyond the models' range is easy to praise after the event. For this reason, these calls get scores, as the winter odds do. Drivers

  • Polar vortex: a major sudden stratospheric warming started on 10 November. NASA GEOS shows -2.9 m/s at 60N and 10 hPa on 11 November, a record low. The wind is 3 to 4 m/s on 14 November, below the ERA5 lowest for the date of 9.2 m/s. It is the earliest warming in the NASA MERRA-2 record since 1979/80.
  • Depth: the two measures disagree. The 50 hPa wind stays westerly at 7 to 12 m/s, near the 10th percentile. The 100 hPa cap reached +2.19 sd on 4 November. It returns above +1 sd in 61% of ECMWF ENS, 63% of AIFS ENS, 81% of GEPS and 13% of GEFS members. So the event is deep.

  • Shape: the ECMWF ENS mean puts the vortex off the pole towards Scandinavia on 19 November. On 24 November, it stretches the vortex from Canada to Siberia. It is back on the pole on 29 November. Wave-2 ridges near the Urals and Alaska tilt west with height, but the wind rises, so this is a stretch, not a split.
  • Wave forcing: the 100 hPa heat flux peaked at 46.4 K m/s on 29 October. A second pulse of 27 to 32 K m/s comes on 21 to 23 November, mostly wave 2. It meets a vortex that rebuilds, so it reverses the vortex in no member.

  • Sea temperatures: the North Sea is +0.77 degC, cooler than my last outlook, so coast snow stays marginal. The tripole is -0.61, a weak positive-NAO sign. The Barents and Kara Seas are +1.73 degC, linked to a weaker vortex.

Polar vortex: will the winds reverse?

This section shows the speed of the stratospheric polar vortex: the average west-to-east wind around the 60°N circle, about 30 km up (10 hPa). Below zero, the wind is easterly, and this is a sudden stratospheric warming (SSW). After about two thirds of SSWs, the chance of blocked, cold weather here increases 2–4 weeks later. The rest do not reach the weather. A strong vortex usually gives mild westerlies.

Early warning signs

A sudden warming starts when waves from the lower atmosphere push up into the vortex. Then a sharp warming shows over the pole, before the winds reverse. These two charts look for both signs.

Temperature over the pole, 10 hPa

This is the average over 60–90°N. A jump of 20 °C or more in a few days shows a warming under way. The grey band is the normal for the date.

Wave energy that goes up (heat flux, 100 hPa)

This is the heat that the waves carry poleward around 60°N each day (K m/s). Large or long pulses hit the vortex and often make it weaker 1–2 weeks later.

The heat flux by wave, 100 hPa

This chart divides the heat flux above into the part that wave 1 carries and the part that wave 2 carries. A wave-1 pulse usually pushes the vortex off the pole. A wave-2 pulse stretches the vortex and can split it in two. Pick a period to see the data since 1 October, or the weeks around the last sudden warming.

East Pacific Oscillation (EPO)

The EPO compares the 500 hPa height near Alaska (55–65°N) with the height farther south (20–35°N), both at 160–125°W. A positive EPO means a trough near Alaska and mild Pacific air. A negative EPO means a ridge over Alaska. This often sends cold air into North America and can make the upward wave flux into the vortex stronger. This page calculates the index itself, so it can differ a little from the EPO on other sites. The days match the heat flux charts above, so you can compare when the two change.
